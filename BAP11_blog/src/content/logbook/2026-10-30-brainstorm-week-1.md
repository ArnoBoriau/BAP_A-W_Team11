Our brainstorm started with two questions:

> Who comes to the event, and what are their goals and obstacles?

For each visitor we wrote down who they are, what they do, what they want and what stands in their way. That gave us **five visitor groups**, each with their own research question:

1. **The architect arriving alone** wants to connect with peers, but feels awkward on their own.
2. **The professional with a planned route** walks straight past the walls, focused on the booths.
3. **The interior designer arriving in a group** is already in conversation and doesn't turn to the walls.
4. **The visitor during peak hours** can't see the walls or stop at them, because the crowd pushes them on. Will they remember to come back?
5. **The visitor who only visits one hall** can't see the other wall and doesn't want to walk the 90 m.

## From questions to How Might We's

For each group, we turned the obstacles into **How Might We questions**. That gave us more than 30 HMWs, for example:

- HMW let the walls connect people who don't know each other yet?
- HMW make the walls work for people who are just passing by?
- HMW make the walls a recurring part of the visit, not a one-time stop?
- HMW make the walls something people experience together?
- HMW make two walls 90 m apart feel like one experience?

## Grouping them along the visitor journey

We grouped the HMWs into five themes:

- **A. Breaking the ice** (connect)
- **B. Catching attention in motion** (notice)
- **C. The walls as part of the visit**
- **D. Shared and collective experience** (experience together)
- **E. Bridge the two halls** (continue into the visit and the other hall)

Putting these themes in order showed us how a visitor moves past the walls. We found that the order is different depending on who you are:

- **Solo visitors:** notice → connect → experience together → continue. They first need to connect with a stranger before they can share the experience, so **connection is the entry point**.
- **Groups:** notice → experience together → connect → continue. They already have company, so they share the experience first and only open up to others afterwards. Here **connection is the result**.

The journey also raised a new question: what about visitors who come back a second time? If the walls always look the same, we lose their attention. So we added new HMWs to theme C, such as *HMW make the walls different every time a visitor passes?* and *HMW make the second encounter richer than the first?*

> Everything up to this point is based on **assumptions**. We will check them with interviews and surveys in the next phase.

## A day of brainstorming on our own

Then each of us spent a day brainstorming alone, so everyone could follow their own ideas first.

- **Amber** used a mind map of associations, a superhero brainstorm, forced associations, a mash-up and Crazy 8s. Ideas included a shared technical drawing that turns into a 3D building on the other wall, floor triangles that change the visuals the more people stand on them, and connecting the inside and outside of a building.
- **Arno** collected random insights, such as *"the wall makes the visitors the main attraction"*, *"walls talk to each other"* and *"visualise human connection"*. He grouped them into three directions: **Living structures**, **Collective architecture** (*can a crowd become a design tool?*) and **Materiality** (*glass that bends like fabric, stone that behaves like water*).
- **Emelie** started from insights: visitors keep walking, so the walls must impress straight away without explanation; architects quickly see when something is just decoration; the 90 m between the walls can be a strength; and over two days the installation can evolve. From these she came up with **Studio & Site**: one wall is the studio where a design is made, the other is the site where it gets built. Her top 3 HMWs were *let the walls do the introducing*, *make the crowd part of the experience* and *make the two walls feel like one piece of architecture despite 90 m*.

## Bringing it together

The next day (29/10), we came back together and put all our ideas side by side. We looked for overlaps and combined the strongest ideas into **one concept: Fenestra** (Latin for *window*).

The two walls become neighbouring buildings, 90 m apart. Visitors bring them to life with their presence. When two strangers stand at the same spot in different halls, a window opens and they see each other.

On 30/10 we added the idea of a corridor that focuses on materials, light, plants and bricks: the hall becomes the hallway of a house you walk into.
